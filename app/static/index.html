How the checker works

  1. Input. The original clause and its simplification are read together, as one sequence, by a multilingual transformer (XLM-RoBERTa) fine-tuned for this task.
  2. Three outputs. One shared encoder feeds three heads: a score from 1 to 10, a decision (Preserved ≥ 6.5, Not preserved ≤ 2, otherwise Partially preserved) and nine legal error types.
  3. Rule check. A transparent legal diff compares obligations and permissions (shall / may), negations, exceptions (unless, provided that), conditions, numbers and dates, scope words and the parties.
  4. Word attributions. Integrated Gradients traces the score back to individual words: red words pulled the score down.

Training data

FrJUDGE (Beauchemin et al., EMNLP 2025): 297 French insurance clauses and simplifications, each scored by five law students. The pairs were translated into English and Hindi with NLLB-200, keeping the human scores, and extended with identical (score 10) and unrelated (score 1) pairs and rule-made legal edits of English and Hindi clauses.

Limits

  • English and Hindi scores were learned from translated French judgments; our team's own annotations are the next step.
  • The model is weakest on changed numbers and dates; the rule check covers those.
  • Long inputs are cut at 256 sub-word tokens.
  • An aid for checking simplifications, not legal advice.
